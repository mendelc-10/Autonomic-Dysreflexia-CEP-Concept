Emergency Response Protocols and Volunteer Responsibilities

In this section we will go over the treatments for autonomic dysreflexia and why they work, and your responsibilities as a MacWheelers volunteer!

What can be done to treat, or stop, an autonomic dysreflexia episode?

Many instances of autonomic dysreflexia can be stopped at the source. This means that if the trigger for the attack can be identified adn resolved, then the attack should end. Unfortunately, this is not always the case, hence it is important to continuously monitor an individual's blood pressure after removing the trigger to ensure the attack ends in full. There are a few common triggers of autonomic dysreflexia episodes in individuals with upper spinal cord injuries:

  • Bladder
  • Bowel
  • Tight clothing and pressure sores
  • A pain or itch below the injury
Let's look at the underlying pathway for these triggers!

Blood Pressure Modulation

These triggers all provide sensory input below the site of the injury: bladder and bowel distention produce input from stretch receptors; tight clothing and pressure sores result in haptic input; and pain and itch cause nociceptive input. In these instances, that input is not registered by higher processing areas—a consequence of pathway disruption from the injury. As a result, it becomes what we refer to as 'noxious stimulus'.

Sympathetic preganglionic neurons (SPN) are tonically active neurons in the spinal cord that provide modulatory input to peripheral sympathetic chain ganglia and adrenal medulla. Peripheral sympathetic chain ganglia innervate blood vessels and adrenal medulla secretes epinephrine and norepinephrine—both important for directly and indirectly modulating blood pressure. The SPNs receive input from the central nervous system, and by providing input to those areas are able to regulate blood pressure. If the spinal cord is damaged the SPN input pathways can be disrupted, preventing descending modulatory input and causing the SPNs to become 'hyper-excitable' (Eldahan & Rabchevsky, 2018).

Coming back to our 'noxious stimulus' from above, that sensory input can trigger reflexive SPN firing that cannot be modulated. The SPNs are already 'hyper-excitable' and activity of the neurons is important for blood pressure regulation, so uncontrolled reflexive activity results in what is called an episode of AD.

Response Protocols

Now, you may be wondering how you can apply what you have just learned during your volunteer experience!

If you know or suspect that a participant is experiencing an AD episode, your primary responsibility as a MacWheelers volunteer is to try and assist the participant, report the attack to a PACE staff, and contact the campus emergency response team (EFRT) and local paramedics. Do not attempt to treat the attack, that is beyond your qualifications and thus responsibilities as a volunteer.

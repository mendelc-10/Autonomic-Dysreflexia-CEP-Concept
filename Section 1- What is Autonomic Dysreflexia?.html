What Causes Autonomic Dysreflexia?

After spinal cord injury, neurons that are no longer receiving structured input can create new synapses with other neurons (Soriano et al., 2025). There are two new types of synapses that commonly form in people with injuries above T6 that create the circuit responsible for causing autonomic dysreflexia. These synapses form between c fibres and a group of long projecting neurons in the lumbo-sacral spine and from those lumbo-sacral neurons onto neurons in the lower thoracic spine that naturally synapse onto sympathetic preganglionic neurons. Sympathetic preganglionic neurons synapse onto postganglionic neurons in the splanchnic ganglia that release norepinephrine onto the smooth muscle cells along the splanchnic vasculature. The norepinephrine binds to alpha 1 adrenergic receptors and causes vascular constriction raising blood pressure. Normally this raise in blood pressure could be counteracted through the action of the parasympathetic nervous system but spinal cord injury tends to disrupt that pathway (Eldahan & Rabchevsky, 2018). This means that blood pressure will stay raised until the stimulus activating the c fibres is removed. This information will help us explain what autonomic dysreflexia is and how it acts to the volunteer audience of our CEP.
